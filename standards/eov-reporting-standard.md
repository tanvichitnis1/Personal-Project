# EOV Standard for Strategic Reports and Presentations

**Status:** Proposed internal standard. Informed by the references reviewed; it does not claim that every global consultancy follows identical rules.

**Core principle:** The standard governs the quality of the argument as well as the appearance of the document. Consistent fonts and imagery make EOV recognisable; clear choices, defensible comparisons and practical recommendations make its work valuable.

## 1. Purpose and governing principle

Every EOV report must help the client understand:

1. What matters.
2. What the analysis establishes.
3. What EOV recommends.
4. Why that recommendation is appropriate.
5. What implementation requires.
6. How progress will be assessed.

**Lead with the recommendation. Support it with analysis. Translate it into action.**

The report must demonstrate professional judgement through the relevance of its evidence, the quality of its reasoning and the specificity of its recommendations.

Volume, technical vocabulary and the number of frameworks used are not measures of quality.

## 2. Distinguish the two deliverables

| Requirement | Full report | Executive presentation |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Complete, durable record of the analysis and strategy | Guide a discussion and secure decisions |
| **Reader experience** | Read independently, at the reader’s own pace | Follow a presenter or scan before a meeting |
| **Structure** | Chapters, connected analysis, exhibits, recommendations and supporting detail | Selected conclusions, decisive exhibits and decisions |
| **Writing** | Complete paragraphs with clear subheadings | Concise statements and limited supporting copy |
| **Evidence** | Sufficient detail to examine the reasoning | Evidence necessary to understand the principal conclusions |
| **Visual approach** | Editorial pages with varied, disciplined layouts | Landscape slides with a clear focal point |
| **Supporting material** | Appendices and detailed comparisons | Appendix for likely questions |
| **Length** | Determined by substantive coverage | Determined by meeting purpose and decision complexity |

The report must not be a presentation placed on portrait pages.

The presentation must not reproduce the full report page by page.

Both must use the same recommendations, definitions, figures and qualifications.

## 3. Establish the argument before designing pages

Before layout begins, prepare a working outline containing:

- The client’s business question.
- EOV’s principal recommendation.
- The three to five arguments supporting it.
- The evidence supporting each argument.
- The alternatives considered.
- The principal trade-offs and risks.
- The actions and decisions that follow.

For each proposed section, state:

> **This section establishes ______, which means ______ for the client.**

Remove or relocate sections that do not support understanding, a recommendation or a practical decision.

Do not begin by allocating an arbitrary number of pages to each framework.

## 4. Standard full-report architecture

Adapt this sequence to the engagement. Preserve its logic rather than forcing every project into identical chapter titles.

| Section | Required content |
|---|---|
| **Cover** | Client, report title, EOV attribution and appropriate confidentiality designation |
| **Executive summary and recommendations** | Main recommendation, strongest supporting conclusions, strategic priorities and decisions required |
| **Contents** | Clear navigation through the report |
| **Business context and ambition** | Organisation, objectives, relevant capabilities, offer and engagement context |
| **Market and competitive assessment** | Relevant external conditions, competitors, alternatives and material patterns |
| **Customer assessment** | Priority audiences, needs, decision routes, selection considerations and experience |
| **Strategic assessment** | Synthesis, options, trade-offs, risks and rationale for the selected direction |
| **Recommended strategy** | Explicit choices and their consequences for the business |
| **Application and implementation** | What the strategy requires from services, people, communication, platforms and processes |
| **Measurement and governance** | Objectives, indicators, ownership, review cadence and learning |
| **Decisions and next steps** | Specific approvals, actions and dependencies |
| **Supporting analysis** | Detailed comparisons, technical material, definitions and source notes |

Keep executive recommendations at the beginning.

Place detailed substantiation in the relevant chapters.

Close the main report with action and decisions. Supporting references may follow.

## 5. Executive summary standard

The executive summary must stand alone for a reader who does not continue through the report.

It must answer:

- What is EOV recommending?
- Why is this the appropriate direction?
- What does it require?
- What are the material trade-offs or conditions?
- What needs to happen next?

Use three to five priorities where possible. Each must contain a decision or meaningful action.

**Preferred construction:**

> **Recommendation → rationale → consequence**

Avoid filling the summary with background, a list of activities completed or a description of the report’s chapters.

Do not introduce findings in the summary that are absent from the body.

Write the executive summary after the substantive argument is established, even though it appears first.

## 6. Voice and language

EOV’s voice is **measured, direct, precise and commercially grounded**.

| Use | Avoid |
|---|---|
| “We recommend positioning…” | Repeated “The client should consider…” |
| “Prioritise…” | “It may potentially be beneficial to…” |
| “The analysis indicates…” | “This proves…” when causation is unestablished |
| “Published offers overlap…” | “All competitors are the same…” |
| “This establishes a clear market role…” | “This guarantees differentiation…” |
| “Implementation requires…” | “Simply implement…” |
| Specific benefits and responsibilities | Unsupported adjectives such as exceptional, seamless or world-class |

Use active voice and concrete verbs.

Explain technical terms when the intended reader needs that explanation.

**Precision matters more than impressive vocabulary.** Do not make straightforward ideas harder to understand.

Vary language naturally. Do not repeat the same verb across multiple headlines.

Use “should” selectively. It is acceptable where a recommendation is conditional, but it must not make the entire report sound tentative.

## 7. Calibrate authority to evidence

Be decisive about EOV’s recommendations and accurate about their evidential basis.

| Statement type | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Verified fact** | State directly and support where appropriate |
| **Organisation’s published claim** | Attribute it |
| **Modelled estimate** | Label it as estimated and identify the relevant basis |
| **Analytical interpretation** | Explain the reasoning |
| **Recommendation** | State clearly as EOV’s recommended action |
| **Illustration or scenario** | Label explicitly; identify material assumptions |
| **Target** | Present as an intended result, not a forecast |
| **Forecast** | Identify assumptions and uncertainty |

Never convert:

- Visibility into commercial success.
- Website features into observed customer behaviour.
- Traffic estimates into market share.
- A competitor’s claim into an independently established fact.
- An attractive process into a defensible competitive advantage.
- An individual’s experience into a new company’s track record.

Client-facing reports must retain qualifications that materially affect interpretation. Keep them concise and close to the relevant statement.

## 8. Research-to-recommendation discipline

Each major analytical argument must contain four elements:

1. **Finding:** What has been established?
2. **Evidence:** What supports it?
3. **Interpretation:** Why does it matter?
4. **Recommendation:** What action follows?

These elements do not need to appear as four labelled boxes on every page. They should form a coherent argument across the section.

A section is incomplete if it provides extensive observations without explaining their significance.

A recommendation is incomplete if the reader cannot understand its supporting rationale.

Maintain detailed traceability internally. In the client report, present readable sources and necessary qualifications.

## 9. Fair comparison rules

Compare organisations, offers and markets on a relevant and consistent basis.

- Explain why each competitor or alternative is included.
- Match customer situations and service scope.
- Separate direct competitors, indirect alternatives and official self-service routes.
- Use comparable periods, geographies and metric definitions.
- Name currencies and units.
- Disclose differences that prevent a like-for-like comparison.
- Distinguish “not observed” from “does not exist.”
- Avoid ranking an organisation’s overall quality from public marketing material.

For pricing, identify the relevant basket: jurisdiction, service, inclusions, exclusions, professional and external fees, tax and applicable period.

Do not add numerical scores merely to make judgement appear objective. Scores require defined criteria, anchors and a stated weighting rationale.

## 10. Strategic completeness

A strategy must make choices.

Depending on scope, establish:

- Priority customers and needs.
- Relevant markets and competitive alternatives.
- Value proposition and positioning.
- Services or offers to prioritise.
- Capabilities required.
- Trade-offs and boundaries.
- Reasons to believe.
- Delivery requirements.
- Application priorities.
- Measurement and ownership.

For brand strategy, additionally cover:

- Purpose.
- Vision.
- Mission.
- Values expressed through behaviour.
- Brand character.
- Brand and service architecture.
- Customer experience principles.
- Brand narrative.
- Messaging.
- Verbal identity.
- Visual direction.
- Claims and proof.

**Messaging and tone express the strategy. They do not replace it.**

## 11. Framework selection

Use recognised methods internally when they improve the analysis. Present the resulting insight in language relevant to the client.

| Analytical need | Suitable approach | Client-facing output |
|---|---|---|
| External change | PESTLE | Market conditions and strategic consequences |
| Competitive pressure | Five forces | Sources of pressure and routes to value |
| Strengths and vulnerabilities | SWOT followed by TOWS | Strategic strengths, challenges and responses |
| Positioning alternatives | Weighted decision matrix | Options, trade-offs and selected direction |
| Risk | Likelihood–impact assessment | Priority exposures, controls and responsibilities |
| Customer understanding | Segmentation, personas and journeys | Customer needs, decision routes and experience priorities |
| Organisational alignment | Capability or operating-model assessment | Changes required to deliver the strategy |
| Prioritisation | Impact–effort assessment | Sequenced actions |
| Consequence testing | Second-order thinking and inversion | Downstream effects and failure prevention |

Use a framework only when its inputs are sufficient and its output changes understanding or action.

Do not include every model merely to demonstrate effort.

## 12. Headline hierarchy

Different levels have different jobs.

| Level | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Report title** | Define the engagement | Market, Competitive and Brand Strategy |
| **Chapter heading** | Orient the reader | Customers and appointment decisions |
| **Analytical heading** | State the finding | Published prices cover materially different scopes |
| **Exhibit title** | State what the visual establishes | Headline fees exclude different elements of establishment |
| **Subheading** | Organise the explanation | Continuing obligations |
| **Caption** | Explain an image or detail | Illustrative service application |

Avoid relying on generic headings such as “Analysis,” “Insights” or “Recommendations” without a specific statement beneath them.

Chapter headings can be descriptive. Every page does not need a long action headline.

## 13. Editorial page system

Use a controlled family of layouts.

| Page type | Required treatment |
|---|---|
| **Cover** | Strong visual, clear title hierarchy, restrained branding |
| **Executive summary** | Short introduction and clearly prioritised recommendations |
| **Chapter opening** | Coherent imagery, chapter heading and introductory argument |
| **Analysis page** | Connected paragraphs, subheadings and deliberate whitespace |
| **Exhibit page** | Finding-led title, legible visual, explanation and source |
| **Comparative spread** | Consistent comparison structure across related pages |
| **Example panel** | Clearly distinguished competitor example or illustration |
| **Recommendation page** | Action, rationale, consequences and material conditions |
| **Reference page** | Detailed material with consistent navigation |

Do not force every page into cards, columns or a full-page table.

Allow arguments to continue across pages. Avoid unnecessary breaks that fragment reading.

## 14. EOV visual identity

Use approved EOV assets and colour specifications.

- White provides the main reading background.
- Black and dark grey carry primary text.
- Approved EOV cyan provides selective emphasis.
- Pale neutral or approved cyan tints may distinguish supporting panels.
- Orange may remain within suitable imagery; do not use it for report typography, tables or analytical graphics under this standard.
- Place the EOV logo cleanly, without an added blue background shape.
- Preserve logo proportions and approved clear space.
- Avoid decorative gradients, heavy shadows and unnecessary rounded containers.

Do not borrow another consultancy’s colours, proprietary fonts or signature branding.

## 15. Typography and readability

Use **Arial as the default for this reporting system**. Calibri is an acceptable alternative where agreed. Do not mix them within one deliverable.

These are starting ranges; verify them in the actual format.

| Element | A4 report | Landscape presentation |
|---|---:|---:|
| Cover title | 30–40 pt | 36–48 pt |
| Chapter heading | 26–30 pt | 30–36 pt |
| Section/page heading | 17–22 pt | 28–34 pt |
| Subheading | 12–14 pt | 20–24 pt |
| Main text | 10.5–11.5 pt | 18–22 pt |
| Table/exhibit text | Usually 9–10.5 pt | Usually 14–18 pt |
| Notes and sources | 8–9 pt | 10–12 pt |

Rules:

- Establish hierarchy through size, weight and spacing.
- Use bold selectively.
- Keep body text left-aligned.
- Avoid long passages in capitals or italics.
- Use comfortable line spacing; begin around 1.2–1.35 times the type size.
- Keep report text columns at a comfortable reading width.
- Prevent isolated final words, awkward heading breaks and headings stranded at page bottoms.
- Never solve overflow by repeatedly shrinking text.

If content does not fit, edit, restructure or split it.

## 16. Grid and spacing

Maintain consistent margins and alignment across the document.

For A4, begin with approximately **18–22 mm margins**, adjusting for the layout and intended binding. Full-bleed imagery may extend beyond the reading grid.

- Use repeatable spacing increments.
- Align headings, paragraphs and exhibits to a shared grid.
- Provide greater space between sections than between related elements.
- Keep titles clearly separated from body content.
- Maintain consistent table-cell padding.
- Keep footers and sources outside the main reading area.
- Check spacing optically as well as numerically.

Whitespace must clarify relationships. It must not compensate for missing content or create unnecessary pages.

## 17. Imagery

Imagery establishes atmosphere, chapter rhythm and visual coherence.

Use one consistent family of:

- Colour treatment.
- Lighting.
- Crop.
- Material or photographic character.
- Subject matter.
- Contrast.

Use images principally on the cover, chapter openings and selected visual pauses. Do not add an image to every page.

Every image must have a purpose:

- Establish context.
- Introduce a theme.
- Make an abstract idea tangible.
- Demonstrate an application.
- Provide a deliberate pause.

Avoid generic handshakes, staged boardrooms, unrelated skylines, visible third-party branding and artificial-looking people.

Use licensed photography or appropriately approved generated imagery. Do not present generated scenes as documentary evidence of the client’s operations.

For full-page assets, target sufficient resolution for the intended output—typically approximately 300 ppi for print. Check the final crop at full size.

## 18. Charts, diagrams and infographics

Choose the visual according to the question.

| Question | Preferred visual |
|---|---|
| How do alternatives compare? | Bar chart, dot plot or comparison matrix |
| Where do published offers sit? | Offer map with defined dimensions |
| How does a process work? | Process diagram |
| How does the customer experience unfold? | Journey map |
| What contributes to a total? | Stacked bar or waterfall |
| What changes over time? | Line chart or timeline |
| Which risks require attention? | Risk matrix with explained criteria |
| What takes priority? | Ranked list or prioritisation matrix |
| How are services related? | Architecture diagram |
| What exact details differ? | Table |

Every exhibit must:

- Answer a specific question.
- Have a clear title.
- Define units, categories and scope.
- Use legible labels.
- Explain material assumptions.
- Include an appropriate source.
- Support the surrounding argument.

Do not invent quantities or use arbitrary coordinates to create an impressive-looking map.

Distinguish a map of **published offer emphasis** from a map of **customer perceptions**. The latter requires customer evidence.

Use editable charts, shapes and labels for precise analytical visuals. AI-generated images must not carry factual charts, exact labels or data relationships.

## 19. Table standard

Use tables where exact comparison matters.

- Use descriptive column headings.
- Left-align text and right-align numerical values.
- State units in headers.
- Apply consistent decimal precision.
- Use light horizontal rules.
- Avoid heavy cell grids.
- Use restrained header fills.
- Repeat headers across continued pages.
- Avoid splitting a row where it harms comprehension.
- Keep qualifiers close to the values they qualify.

A table should help readers compare. If it becomes a collection of long paragraphs, change the layout or divide it by question.

## 20. Examples, quotations and proof

Separate main analysis from supporting material.

- Use neutral panels for case examples.
- Attribute quotations accurately.
- Label hypothetical illustrations clearly.
- Distinguish proposed applications from existing assets.
- Use testimonials only with appropriate permission.
- Identify whose experience or results are being described.

Never create a client story, testimonial, operating result or quotation to fill a design gap.

## 21. Client-facing exclusions

Remove:

- Internal research codes and filenames.
- Working comments and editorial instructions.
- Unresolved drafting placeholders.
- Internal confidence tags.
- References to previous drafts or merged documents.
- Internal staffing, supplier or margin discussions.
- Framework names included solely to demonstrate methodology.
- Labels such as “EOV implications.”
- Process commentary about how much work was performed.

Use client-facing language such as:

**Finding · Strategic significance · Recommendation · Priority action**

Public sources, necessary methodological context and material qualifications remain visible.

## 22. Presentation standard

The presentation must allow leadership to grasp the recommendation quickly and interrogate its rationale.

Typical sequence:

1. Recommendation.
2. Principal findings.
3. Customer and competitive logic.
4. Strategic choice and trade-offs.
5. Recommended brand platform.
6. Practical applications.
7. Implementation priorities.
8. Decisions required.

On each slide:

- Establish one dominant takeaway.
- Use one principal exhibit or a tightly related group.
- Keep text readable from meeting distance.
- Remove prose already explained by the visual.
- Retain essential qualifications.
- Put detailed backup in the appendix.
- Use speaker notes for supplementary explanation.

Do not impose an arbitrary slide count. Edit to the meeting’s purpose and available time.

## 23. Implementation and measurement

Recommendations need practical consequences.

Where implementation falls within scope, identify:

- Action.
- Intended outcome.
- Responsible role.
- Timing.
- Dependencies.
- Success measure.
- Review point.

Separate:

- **Deliverables:** what will be produced.
- **Outcomes:** what is intended to change.
- **Indicators:** how change will be assessed.

Do not invent budgets, performance targets or returns. Identify assumptions and recommend how targets will be established where no baseline exists.

## 24. Quality assurance before delivery

| Review | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| **Strategic logic** | Recommendations follow from the analysis; alternatives and trade-offs are addressed |
| **Completeness** | Agreed scope is covered without unnecessary repetition |
| **Factual accuracy** | Names, services, jurisdictions and claims are checked |
| **Numerical accuracy** | Calculations, totals, units, currencies and counts reconcile |
| **Evidence quality** | Facts, estimates, claims and interpretations are distinguished |
| **Editorial quality** | Clear language, consistent terminology and no contradictory positions |
| **Visual quality** | Every page is rendered and inspected for readability, spacing and clipping |
| **Exhibit quality** | Labels, axes, sources and qualifications are correct |
| **Cross-document consistency** | Report and presentation use the same facts and recommendations |
| **Delivery quality** | Files open, links work, editable elements remain usable and versions are clear |

Automated checks support visual inspection; they do not replace it.

Do not describe a document as verified until the relevant checks have been completed.

## 25. Final acceptance test

Before releasing any EOV strategic report, ask:

> **Can the client understand the recommendation, examine its basis and act on it without needing EOV to explain missing logic?**

For a brand strategy, add:

> **Can the leadership, advisory, design, website and marketing teams make consistent decisions from this document?**

If either answer is no, the work requires further development.

**Internal rule to retain:**

> EOV reports lead with a clear recommendation, substantiate it through relevant analysis and translate it into practical decisions. Full reports use an editorial reading structure; presentations communicate the decisive argument. Both follow approved EOV branding, use purposeful imagery and accurate analytical exhibits, distinguish evidence from interpretation, and undergo content and visual verification before delivery.
